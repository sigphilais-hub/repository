## Paradigms  

The term **paradigm** has a long history in science dating all the way back to ancient Greek. For instance, Plato used the term in *Timaeus* to refer to the model or pattern that the god Demiurge used to create the cosmos, while Aristotle referred to the term in *Rhetorics* to denote that when two statements are similar, but one is more familiar (knowable) than the other, the former is a paradigm (“paradeigma”). Today, the term paradigm continues to hold a prominent place in the scientific discourse, due to the pioneering work of Thomas Kuhn. In his book *The Structure of Scientific Revolution*, Kuhn showed that various entities such as symbols, models, exemplars, rules, guidelines, heuristics, values, and/or beliefs influence the way we conduct research. Hence, similar to what Plato and Aristotle did, Kuhn showed that the reasoning, actions, and ultimately the everyday work of scientists is based on paradigms.

However, despite showing the importance of paradigms for scientific inquiry and progress, Kuhn did not clearly define the term *paradigm* in the first version of his book. As a result, Kuhn’s work faced strong criticisms as researchers from various disciplines argued that the term was too vague and meant different things to different people. Recognizing this shortcoming, Kuhn himself as well as others attempted to clarify the meaning of the term. What can be distilled from these efforts is that paradigms can be of three different but interrelated forms (i.e., **metaphysical, sociological, and artefactual**).

Within IS research, Banville and Landry’s (1989) article about the evolution of our field as a scientific discipline was amongst the first to discuss the notion of (metaphysical) paradigms. A focus that persisted for many years, with subsequent IS research on paradigms remaining largely confined to the exploration of paradigms of the metaphysical form. Yet, realizing that undue focus on metaphysical paradigms places limits on the advancement of our field, some IS researchers have recently reemphasized the critical importance of the three forms of paradigms (i.e., metaphysical, sociological, and artefactual) to develop theories (Hassan and Mingers 2018). Still, work is needed if we are to develop an understanding of sociological and artefactual paradigms that is as elaborate as our understanding of metaphysical paradigms.

The following references move us through this historical take on our understanding of paradigms within social science and the IS field. The journey begins with Kuhn’s (1962, 1970) seminal works and Masterman’s insightful identification of the various forms of paradigms. It continues with the works of Hassan (2014) and Hassan and Mingers (2018) that depict the evolution of the IS field’s understanding of the term paradigm and ends with the work of Marchildon and Hadaya (2023) that examines the importance of considering paradigms when theorizing and trying to make sense of our world. Hence, coming full circle and revealing the importance and roles of paradigms within scientific inquiries.
